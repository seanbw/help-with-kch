A Story of Sickle Cell Anaemia, Chronic Pain and Being Ignored

My experience of living with sickle cell anaemia, chronic pain and trying to have my concerns heard.

What do you do when you are no longer able to function because you are constantly in pain and difficulty, yet those who can help refuse to act? For the past two years, I have complained repeatedly that I am unable to function because the Trust's opioid of choice, Tapentadol, has had little or no noticeable impact on my pain.

What it does have, however, is a side effect that I can only describe as dangerous. No matter the time or place, it has the ability to switch me off as if someone has turned off a light bulb. At first, I thought it was simply me, but after taking the medication for almost a decade, I began to recognise that this problem coincided directly with the change in my medication.

It is only recently that I began to focus seriously on Tapentadol and its side effects. Prior to being switched, I had been taking Oxycodone. It worked well. I had a 30mg breakthrough dose and a modified-release dose every twelve hours to manage background pain.

Looking back, I wish I had never agreed to the change. Although I wanted the switch at the time because the 30mg breakthrough dose made me drowsy, I could simply have asked for a lower dose. Instead, I agreed to change medications and I have regretted that decision almost every day since.

The Oxycodone regime consisted of two forms: immediate-release tablets and Modified Release (M/R) tablets. The M/R formulation, sometimes written as MR, CR or prolonged-release, releases the drug gradually over approximately twelve hours rather than all at once. I took Oxycodone for nearly two decades. Its only significant side effect was drowsiness. If I was inactive for long periods, I would eventually fall asleep.

Since starting Tapentadol, I have steadily lost control of my life. I eventually lost the job I was desperately trying to hold onto, and with it the last semblance of normality. The indignities and losses that followed have been relentless. Today, what stands before you is only a shadow, a very pale shadow, of the person I once was.

Perhaps most baffling of all was the response from my GP Surgery. You might expect that when specialist services fail in their responsibilities, a General Practitioner would carry out an independent assessment. Yet despite numerous letters, emails and recorded deliveries, there was no meaningful response. No acknowledgement. No action. It felt as though both the Pain Department and my GP Surgery had vanished from the world entirely.

I found myself living with a level of pain unlike anything I had previously experienced.

In many ways I have been fortunate. I was diagnosed with Sickle Cell Anaemia by my great-uncle in Nigeria. He confirmed my condition to my parents and recognised the many challenges I faced, including chronic anaemia, kidney complications and curvature of the spine.

Throughout my childhood he took a close interest in my health and ensured I received treatment whenever I became unwell. As I grew older, I was fortunate to have many dedicated doctors involved in my care. Pain control was always available. Eventually, I came under the care of an outstanding Professor of Medicine who happened to have supervised my brother, who had become a doctor himself.

The care I received was consistently excellent. Later, while working, I had access to respected private hospitals and specialists. Whether through public or private healthcare, effective pain management was never a major problem until a newly appointed consultant took over my case.

Then everything changed.

To make matters worse, my ability to drive became increasingly compromised. In the interests of both my own safety and that of others, I voluntarily stopped driving. The effects of the medication were so pervasive that I could no longer justify being behind the wheel.

As a result, I lost freedoms that most people take for granted. I lost mobility, independence, enjoyment and opportunities. My positive outlook slowly disappeared. The attitude that said, "You can't keep a good man down," faded away. Gradually, I became little more than a zombie. In many ways I felt dead, even though I was still breathing.

But things would become worse. Much worse.

That, however, is a story for another day.

Hello. My name is Sean Bankole-Williams. I suffer from Sickle Cell Anaemia. This is the story of how I was failed by the people and institutions entrusted with my care.
